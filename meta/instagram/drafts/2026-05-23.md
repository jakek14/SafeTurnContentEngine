Your MCA isn't a loan. That legal distinction is why it can charge 100% or more annually — legally.

MCAs are structured as "purchases of future receivables." You're selling a portion of future revenue at a discount, not taking on debt.

That classification puts MCAs outside most state usury laws. Traditional loans have rate caps. Purchases don't.

This isn't a loophole. It's how the product was built.

Courts are now testing whether a contract actually functions as a purchase or just calls itself one. If repayment is required regardless of earnings, some courts treat it as a loan.

If you have an MCA, you have a contract designed to exist outside the lending system. Knowing what you actually signed is where restructuring starts.
