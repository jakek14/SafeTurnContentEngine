You took the advance to bridge a gap.

The advance became the gap.

It makes sense at the time. Cash is tight for a specific reason — slow receivables, a vendor payment, a stretch before a big deposit clears. The advance solves the immediate problem.

Then a few hundred dollars leave your account every business day.

Not when things are slow. Every business day. Including the days revenue is strong.

The pull doesn't respond to what week you're having. It just goes.

Six weeks later, you're back in a cash crunch. Not because business is bad — because the daily pull has been drawing down working capital before you could retain it. The same timing mismatch that prompted the first advance is back, running parallel to the payment obligation.

That's when the second advance starts to seem reasonable.

Here's the reframe:

The advance wasn't a bridge. It was a commitment to a daily payment that competes with every other operating obligation — indefinitely, until the structure changes.

More revenue doesn't break the cycle. Working harder doesn't break it. The cycle runs until the obligation underneath it changes.

Businesses that escape MCA debt aren't the ones who outrun the pull schedule. They're the ones who restructure the obligation before the second advance makes it harder.

If you're on your second advance — or wondering how you got back to the same place you were before the first one — that's not bad luck.
