You paid off the MCA. The bank still said no.

That's not a coincidence. That's the UCC-1 lien.

When you accepted the advance, the lender filed a UCC-1 financing statement with your state. It's a public record declaring a security interest in your business assets — receivables, inventory, equipment. All of it, in most cases.

That lien stays on file until it's formally released. And lenders aren't always quick to file the termination paperwork — even after you've paid in full.

Here's what that means in practice:

When a bank or SBA lender runs due diligence on your business, they find that open UCC filing. To an underwriter, it signals another party already holds a claim on the collateral they'd want to secure against.

Most bank lenders won't advance money behind an open blanket lien. SBA loans, specifically, require UCC clearance before approval.

So even if the balance is zero — if the UCC-3 termination was never filed, you're still locked out.

What to do:

Pull your state's UCC records. Search your business name as the debtor. If an open filing is listed, contact the original lender and request a UCC-3 termination statement. Get it in writing. Then verify the termination was actually filed — not just promised.

This doesn't happen automatically. You have to initiate it.

If banks keep declining without a clear explanation, an open UCC filing might be the entire reason.
