Your MCA agreement probably has a clause that can reduce your daily payment. Almost no one knows it's there.

It's called a reconciliation clause.

MCAs are legally structured as "purchases of future receivables" — not loans. That classification is what exempts them from state interest rate caps in most states. But to maintain it, the repayment has to be genuinely tied to your actual revenue.

That's where the reconciliation clause comes in.

If your real revenue drops below what the lender assumed when you signed, you can formally request a payment adjustment — down to a level that reflects what you're actually collecting.

Here's what most owners miss:

— The clause doesn't activate automatically. You have to invoke it.
— Invoking it means notifying the lender in writing and documenting the revenue decline.
— If the lender refuses a valid reconciliation request, they may be in breach of contract.

That last point has real weight. Courts have found that an MCA without a meaningful, functioning reconciliation clause — or where the lender ignores a legitimate request — may no longer qualify as a "purchase of receivables." The agreement can be reclassified as a loan, potentially subject to state usury laws.

Pull out your MCA agreement and search for "reconciliation." Most contracts have it. Many owners have never looked for it.

If your revenue has dropped since you signed — the daily payment you're carrying may not have to be as fixed as it appears.
