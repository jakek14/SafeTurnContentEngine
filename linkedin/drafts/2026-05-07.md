Most MCA agreements have a clause called a Confession of Judgment.

Most business owners who signed one have never heard those words.

Here's what it means in plain English:

If you default on your advance, the lender can file a court judgment against your business — without notifying you first. Without giving you a chance to contest it.

In states that still allow it, that process can take 24 to 48 hours.

What that often looks like in practice: your bank account is frozen before you know a judgment was filed. The first signal tends to be a declined payment — not a legal notice.

Not every MCA lender uses this aggressively. Most don't. But the mechanism is in the contract, and it changes what "default" actually means for a business that has signed one.

A few things worth knowing:

New York banned Confessions of Judgment against out-of-state borrowers in 2019. Massachusetts and Florida have eliminated them from commercial contracts entirely. But in many states, they're still standard MCA practice.

MCA agreements are often signed quickly, under pressure, with attention on how fast the capital arrives. The legal terms get understood later — sometimes much later.

Understanding what you signed changes the conversation. It changes what options exist, what leverage looks like, and what the right move is if payments become difficult.

That conversation is easier to have before a default than after.
