Almost every MCA agreement includes a personal guarantee.

Almost no one reads that section before signing.

What it means: if the business can't pay — you do. Personally.

When an MCA lands in a business account and gets treated as "business capital," the whole thing feels like a business obligation. The personal guarantee clause in the contract says otherwise.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

If the business defaults and the lender pursues collection, they can come after personal assets. Savings accounts. Real estate. Not just what's inside the business.

This doesn't happen in every default. Most lenders use the guarantee as negotiating leverage — the threat of enforcement is more useful to them than the cost of it. But the mechanism is real.

It's in the contract most owners signed in a 24–48 hour approval window.

A few things worth knowing:

Some guarantees are unlimited — meaning the lender can pursue the full balance plus fees and legal costs. Some are limited to your ownership percentage in the business.

If you've stacked multiple advances, each with its own guarantee, the exposure compounds. Each lender has an independent path to collection.

Understanding your full exposure changes what a negotiation looks like. It changes what you're protecting, what you're offering, and what settlement terms actually make sense.

The conversation is easier to have when you know what you signed.
