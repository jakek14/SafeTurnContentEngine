Most MCA contracts include a clause that lets your lender skip the courthouse and go directly to judgment. No trial. No prior notice.

It's called a Confession of Judgment — or COJ.

Here's how it works:

When you sign an MCA agreement, you're often signing a pre-authorized judgment at the same time. If the lender decides you've defaulted — whether you missed a payment or triggered a technical clause — they can file that judgment without notifying you first.

The judgment is entered. A restraining notice goes to your bank.

That's often the first sign something happened.

In states like New York, this can move within 24–48 hours of filing. Once the restraining notice hits your bank, payroll doesn't process. Vendor payments don't go out. The account is frozen while you figure out what you signed.

This isn't hypothetical. In January 2025, the New York AG announced a $1.065 billion judgment and settlement against Yellowstone Capital — one of the country's largest MCA funders — partly over COJ practices.

The clause is legal in commercial finance. It's in the document you signed to get funded.

If you have an MCA, pull out the agreement and search for "Confession of Judgment." If it's in there, you pre-authorized a judgment against your own business the day you signed.

That's worth knowing before it matters.
