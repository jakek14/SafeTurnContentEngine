Most business owners assume a lender has to take them to court before touching their accounts. An MCA agreement with a Confession of Judgment clause doesn't require that.

Here's what that means.

When you signed the MCA, you may have pre-authorized a judgment against yourself. If payments stop, the funder files that pre-signed document with a court clerk — no hearing, no notice, no chance to respond. The judgment is entered, often within days.

From there: accounts can be frozen. Wages garnished. Assets seized. Before you know a judgment was filed.

This isn't unusual. It's a collection mechanism used routinely in MCA defaults.

Most states that permit it require no warning before filing. You don't get a letter, a call, or a countdown. The first sign is usually a call from your bank.

New York prohibited out-of-state COJs in 2019. Connecticut is reconsidering its laws after NPR reported on this in March 2026. But if your MCA agreement is governed by a state where enforcement is still active, the clause can be used.

What to do:

Pull your MCA agreement. Search for "Confession of Judgment," "consent to judgment," or "cognovit note." If the clause is there, you want to know that before payments get tight — not after.

The clause doesn't disappear if you don't read it.
