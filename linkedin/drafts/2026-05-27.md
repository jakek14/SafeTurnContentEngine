New York just gave small businesses a new legal shield against aggressive MCA practices. Most owners don't know it exists.

On February 17, 2026, New York's FAIR Business Practices Act took effect — the most significant update to the state's core consumer protection statute in over four decades.

For the first time, the law empowers the New York Attorney General to pursue businesses for "unfair" and "abusive" practices — not just deceptive ones. And those protections now explicitly extend to small businesses, not just individual consumers.

What that means for MCA holders:

An MCA agreement with fixed daily payments and no genuine functioning reconciliation clause may no longer qualify as a "purchase of future receivables" under this framework. Courts have increasingly found that when repayment is required regardless of actual revenue performance, the product functions more like a loan than a purchase.

If it functions as a loan — and the effective annual rate exceeds New York's 25% criminal usury cap — the agreement may be unenforceable.

Aggressive collection tactics that were routine for years may now meet the FAIR Act's definition of "abusive." The law specifically covers acts that take unreasonable advantage of a business owner's inability to protect their own interests.

This doesn't make every MCA automatically voidable. It means the legal landscape shifted. Tactics that carried no exposure a year ago now carry real enforcement risk for lenders.

If you're carrying MCA debt in New York — or if your lender is incorporated there — this is worth knowing before that conversation gets harder.
