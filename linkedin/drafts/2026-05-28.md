Picture this: A profitable seasonal business. Strong summers. Made every payment on time. Still ended up in the same cash crisis twelve months later.

Here's how it works.

A landscaping company — $1.6M in annual revenue, mostly April through October. In January, the phone stops ringing.

They take an $80,000 MCA to bridge the slow season. Factor rate 1.3, so $104,000 total payback over six months. Roughly $788 a day.

May arrives. Crews are busy. Revenue is up. The daily pull goes out and barely registers — enough is always coming in.

The advance doesn't know what month it is.

Through May, June, July — $788 leaves every morning. The strong months run. The account stays fluid. No missed payments. From the outside: a healthy operation.

October comes. Revenue drops sharply.

The advance is still pulling $788 a day.

November. December. The weakest stretch of the year. Same number as July.

The business spent its strong-revenue months paying an advance that was priced in January. Those good months never had the chance to build a reserve. The slow season arrived without a buffer.

By February, the advance is almost paid off. The account is thin. The equation for getting through spring startup — payroll, materials, equipment — looks identical to twelve months ago.

A profitable year. A business back in the same position it started.

The advance worked exactly as structured. That's the problem.
